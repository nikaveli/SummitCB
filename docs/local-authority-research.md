## 1.1 The asbestos rule almost nobody tells homeowners about ⭐ **strongest single hook**

This is your best content asset. It's counterintuitive, it has hard numbers, it directly affects schedule and budget, and virtually no remodeler markets around it.

Colorado regulates asbestos under Air Quality Control Commission **Regulation 8, Part B**, enforced by CDPHE. <cite index="9-1">An asbestos inspection must be conducted by a Colorado-certified asbestos building inspector prior to renovation or demolition activities that meet regulatory requirements</cite>.

**The trigger levels for a single-family home:**
<cite index="7-1">50 linear feet on pipes; 32 square feet on other surfaces; or the volume equivalent of a 55-gallon drum</cite>. Exceed any one of those and a certified inspection plus professional abatement is legally required.

**Why homeowners get this wrong — the age myth:**
<cite index="3-1">Colorado law requires an asbestos inspection for buildings of any age before renovation or demolition. While the 1989 EPA ban was partially overturned, asbestos is still found in modern materials like imported drywall, joint compound, and floor tiles. In Colorado, age does not grant an exemption from testing requirements</cite>. The only narrow exemption: <cite index="7-1">unless the building was built after October 12, 1988, AND the architect or engineer who built it signs and submits documentation showing that no ACM was specified or used in the construction of the building</cite>.

**The schedule impact nobody budgets for:**
When abatement is required, <cite index="9-1">abatement must be conducted by a Colorado-certified General Abatement Contractor (GAC). The GAC must submit an Asbestos Abatement Notification and Permit Application form to the division along with payment of a permit fee. A ten working-day notification period is required before the work can commence.</cite> For demolition: <cite index="9-1">A Demolition Notification Application Form must be submitted to the division, even if no asbestos was found during the inspection, along with payment of a notification fee.</cite>

**The stakes:**
<cite index="5-1">Understanding when professional abatement is required can protect you from significant regulatory penalties, which can reach $25,000 per day for violations.</cite>

**Content angles:**
- "Why Your 1972 Ranch Needs an Asbestos Test Before We Touch a Wall (Colorado Law, Not Our Rule)"
- "The 32 Square Feet That Can Add Two Weeks to Your Remodel"
- "What a DIY $40 Asbestos Kit Can't Do" — <cite index="3-1">While DIY kits cost $40–$80, they do not include the required certified inspector's report needed for city permits.</cite>
- Where it hides in each era: popcorn ceilings, 9x9 and 12x12 floor tile, tile mastic, duct and pipe wrap, plaster and joint compound, vermiculite attic insulation, roofing felt, siding, boiler insulation. **This is your single best kitchen/bath page insert** — a bathroom gut easily blows past 32 sq ft between the floor tile, the wall board, and the tub surround.

## 1.2 Colorado has no state general contractor license ⭐ **trust-building hook**

<cite index="127-1">Colorado doesn't require a license at the state level for general contractors, but relies on cities and counties to set their own contractor license requirements. That means the licensing process in the city of Denver is different from getting a license in Aurora.</cite> Only specific trades are state-regulated: <cite index="127-1">The Colorado Division of Professions and Occupations oversees licenses for electrical contractors, master electricians, journeyman electricians, plumbing contractors, master plumbers, and journey worker plumbers.</cite>

The practical consequence, and the honest sales argument: <cite index="133-1">Working in multiple Front Range cities means holding multiple municipal licenses.</cite> Denver alone runs four classes — <cite index="133-1">Class A (unlimited), Class B (buildings up to four stories or 50 feet), Class C (one and two-family dwellings and accessory structures), and Class D (specific minor scopes)</cite> — and <cite index="126-1">In Denver, for example, applicants are told to expect anywhere from 14 to 20 business days for application review</cite>.

**Content angle:** "There's No Such Thing as a 'Colorado Licensed Contractor.' Here's What to Actually Ask For." This is a genuinely useful, genuinely differentiating piece — it teaches homeowners a real verification method (ask which *cities* the builder is licensed in, and check that it covers their address) instead of a vague credential. Pairs perfectly with Summit's BBB and local-presence positioning. ⚠ Publish Summit's actual municipal license numbers per city alongside it, or the piece backfires.

## 1.3 Expansive soils — the default Front Range foundation problem

<cite index="55-1">Innocuous as it sounds, Colorado's most significant geologic hazard is expansive or swelling soil — that is, soil laced with layers of various clays. These clays cause more property damage than any other natural hazard.</cite> <cite index="55-1">Annual losses are regularly estimated to be greater that those of tornadoes, floods, earthquakes, and hurricanes combined.</cite>

The mechanics, in numbers you can put in a graphic: <cite index="55-1">The clay mineral responsible generally for expansion is montmorillonite, often called bentonite. A sample of pure montmorillonite may expand up to 15 times its original volume.</cite> The threshold that matters: <cite index="55-1">a 25% increase can be extremely destructive because volume increases of 3% or more are generally considered by engineers to be potentially damaging and require specially designed foundations</cite>.

Scale: <cite index="57-1">One in five Coloradans is affected by expansive soil damage over time, compared to one in ten affected by flooding. Of new homes built on expansive soils, roughly 60% see only minor damage, but 10% see significant damage, some of it beyond repair.</cite> And <cite index="57-1">Lightly loaded, shallow-foundation structures (homes, budget-constrained public buildings) carry the most risk</cite> — which is exactly what a room addition or a detached ADU is.

**The remodel-specific angle other builders miss:** expansive soil damage is driven by *moisture change*, and remodels change moisture. New patios and driveways redirect runoff. New downspout locations concentrate it. New landscaping adds irrigation. A new addition's footing sits at a different depth than the original. This is why Summit should be talking about grading, drainage, and downspout extension as part of remodel scope — not as an upsell, but as damage prevention.

## 1.4 Radon — every county Summit serves is in the EPA's highest zone

<cite index="142-1">The EPA Map of Radon Zones classifies Denver County in Zone 1, the agency's highest predicted indoor-radon category. So is every other Denver Metro county: Adams, Arapahoe, Boulder, Broomfield, Douglas, and Jefferson are all Zone 1.</cite> <cite index="13-1">The Colorado Department of Public Health and Environment says radon is found at elevated levels in one out of every two Colorado homes and recommends testing the lowest living area of the home.</cite>

**The remodel connection — this is the useful part.** Radon levels are not fixed: <cite index="142-1">DDPHE's Denver-specific guidance recommends testing every two to three years because conditions in a home (HVAC changes, settling foundations, remodels, even seasonal pressure differences) can shift radon levels over time.</cite> A remodel that tightens the envelope, changes HVAC, adds a bath fan, or finishes a basement can *raise* radon.

**Sequencing advice that reads as expertise:** test before walls close. <cite index="140-1">Solve radon early: Test before closing walls.</cite> Retrofitting a sub-slab depressurization system into a finished basement is dramatically more invasive than stubbing it in during rough-in.

⚠ Colorado now licenses radon professionals — <cite index="137-1">In June of 2021, Colorado lawmakers approved Colorado House Bill 1195 (HB21-1195), which created a regulatory framework for individuals practicing as radon measurement professionals or radon mitigation professionals. This effectively made Colorado one of the few states requiring radon professionals to be licensed, which is regulated by the Department of Regulatory Agencies (DORA) of Colorado.</cite> If Summit subs this out, verify the sub's DORA license and say so.

Note also: <cite index="142-1">Denver's foundation mix (full basements, finished lower levels, older stone-and-mortar perimeters in some pre-war neighborhoods) gives radon many of those pathways.</cite> Mitigation approach differs by foundation type — great content for a "by era of home" breakdown.

## 1.5 The energy code is moving under everyone's feet

<cite index="109-1">HB22-1362 updates those minimum requirements. The bill directs local governments that have building codes to: Adopt and begin enforcing the 2021 version of the International Energy Conservation Code (IECC), or an energy code that achieves equivalent or better energy performance, when one or more building codes are updated, beginning July 1, 2023. Adopt electric and solar ready requirements as part of energy codes when one or more building codes are updated, beginning July 1, 2023. Adopt and begin enforcing a low energy and carbon code when one or more building codes are updated, beginning July 1, 2026.</cite>

What "electric-ready" actually means in practice — this is the part that surprises homeowners: <cite index="115-1">Electric ready and solar ready code developed by the Energy Code Board, which will include pre-wiring requirements for: Future solar PV or solar thermal installation; Future installation of high efficiency electric appliances in mixed-fuel buildings; Future charging of electric vehicles</cite>.

**The threshold that matters right now:** <cite index="114-1">Model Low Energy and Carbon Code (MLECC): This code minimizes overall carbon dioxide emissions associated with new and renovated homes and commercial buildings. The code is based on the 2024 International Energy Conservation Code, with amendments specific to Colorado. Cities and counties with building codes must adopt this code, or a more stringent code, when adopting or updating any building code after July 1, 2026.</cite>

So: **any of Summit's six jurisdictions that updates its building code from here forward pulls in the MLECC.** That's a live, datable content opportunity — a "which of our cities is on which code, and what it means for your panel" tracker page that you update quarterly. Almost nobody maintains one of these, and it would be a genuine link magnet.

⚠ **Action item:** Build and maintain a code-adoption matrix (jurisdiction × building code edition × energy code edition × effective date). Confirm each city's current adoption directly with its building division before publishing, and date-stamp the page.

**The electrical panel angle:** electric-ready requirements land on top of an existing problem. Postwar Front Range housing stock runs undersized panels — <cite index="15-1">Original panels are undersized for how people live now.</cite> A kitchen remodel in a 1958 ranch can cascade into a service upgrade, which involves the utility, which involves lead time. Great honest-expectations content.

## 1.6 The state housing laws that rewrote local zoning (and the backlash)

Homeowners in these six markets have heard confusing, contradictory things about what they're allowed to build. A clear, neutral explainer is high-value and highly linkable.

**HB24-1152 (ADUs).** <cite index="43-1">On May 13, 2024, Governor Polis signed House Bill (HB) 24-1152, requiring subject jurisdictions to take a variety of actions to encourage and allow the construction of accessory dwelling units (ADUs). All subject jurisdictions must submit a report showing compliance with the law by June 30, 2025.</cite> The substance: <cite index="42-1">HB24-1152: This new state law now requires cities to allow 500-750-square-foot accessory dwelling units (ADUs) wherever single-family homes are allowed.</cite> Scope: <cite index="89-1">The new state law applies to all cities with a population of more than 1,000 that are also part of a metropolitan planning organization, or MPO. A total of 67 towns and cities are subject to the law</cite>. It also killed owner-occupancy mandates — <cite index="98-1">Colorado HB 24-1152 prohibits cities from requiring owner occupancy for ADUs, effective June 30, 2025</cite>.

**HB24-1304 (parking).** <cite index="42-1">HB24-1304: This new state law prohibits cities from requiring minimum parking requirements for multifamily complexes built in the future</cite> in transit service areas.

**HB23-1255 (growth caps).** <cite index="19-1">House Bill 1255, passed by the Colorado General Assembly and signed into law by Gov.</cite> Polis banned residential growth caps and repealed existing ones. <cite index="19-1">Lakewood voted to adhere to the law by repealing its municipal cap. Boulder is set to repeal its cap, too. Golden and Lafayette have so far punted.</cite>

**HB24-1007 (occupancy limits).** <cite index="38-1">A new state law, HB24-1007, prevents cities from regulating the number of people living in a house.</cite>

**The enforcement teeth — important context for the Lakewood story below:** <cite index="26-1">The Polis administration has called out numerous Colorado cities for non-compliance with new state housing laws and has threatened to hold back state grant funding for those municipalities</cite>, and <cite index="35-1">Cities in violation could collectively lose out on hundreds of millions of dollars of state funding</cite>.

**Content angle:** "Can I Build an ADU in [City]? The State Says Yes. Your City Might Say Something Else." Neutral, factual, updated. ⚠ Keep this politically neutral — these are contested local fights and Summit's customers are on both sides. Report what the rules are, not what they should be.

## 1.7 Era-of-home systems reference (the backbone of every city page)

Build this once as a master table, then pull the relevant rows into each city page based on that city's dominant era. Well-documented for the postwar Front Range stock that dominates all six markets:

**Pre-1940 (Denver bungalows/Squares, Olde Town Arvada, Golden's historic districts):**
- Knob-and-tube wiring; no ground; 60-amp or smaller original service
- Unreinforced masonry; brick-and-block or stone-and-mortar foundations
- Balloon framing — no fire blocking, and load paths that surprise people when you open a wall
- Galvanized supply and cast iron waste
- Plaster and lath over dimensional lumber (real 2x4s; nothing is 16" on center)
- Buried coal chutes and, occasionally, abandoned fuel oil tanks
- Lead paint (pre-1978 → EPA RRP rule applies to the contractor)

**1940s–1970s (the dominant stock in Arvada, Wheat Ridge, and Lakewood):**
- <cite index="15-1">Sewer. Cast iron and clay lines from the 1950s and 60s. Scope it. Every time. A line replacement under a mature yard is not cheap and it is not rare.</cite>
- <cite index="15-1">Some homes still have aluminum branch wiring from the late 60s and early 70s builds, which is a real insurance conversation.</cite>
- <cite index="15-1">Windows. A lot of these homes still wear original single-pane aluminum.</cite>
- <cite index="15-1">Asbestos and lead. Popcorn ceilings, floor tile, pipe wrap, original paint. Standard for the era, manageable, but budget for it if you are renovating.</cite>
- <cite index="15-1">Flat roofs. Beautiful, and a maintenance item forever.</cite> (Especially relevant in Paramount Heights, Wheat Ridge.)
- Swamp coolers on original roof curbs; converting to refrigerated A/C means new electrical, new refrigerant lines, and patching the curb
- Also expect: Federal Pacific Stab-Lok panels, undersized returns, no insulation in exterior walls, and hail-history roofs

**Post-1990s west Arvada, Candelas, Leyden Rock:** <cite index="79-1">homes are recent, systems are modern, and remodeling is about layout and finish rather than repair</cite> — but HOA and metro-district design review becomes the primary friction instead.

**Content angle:** "What We Expect to Find Behind the Walls of a [decade] [City] Home." One per city, per dominant era. This is the highest-converting format in remodel content because it's specific, it's honest, and it pre-frames change orders.

# LAYER 2 — COUNTY

## 2.1 Denver City & County

**The permit timeline is the story.** This is the single most searched, least honestly answered question in Denver remodeling.

<cite index="83-1">In April 2025, the City and County of Denver launched the Denver Permitting Office (DPO) to address long-standing inefficiencies in the permitting and plan review process.</cite> <cite index="83-1">The City and County of Denver aims for a target timeline of 180 days for all building permit approvals. That 180 days or about 6 months has been the defining feature of the new Denver Permit Office. In the past, that timeframe has been upwards of 300 days or more due to increased demand, staffing issues, and general process deficiencies within Denver's Community Planning and Development Department.</cite>

Reality as of mid-2026: <cite index="82-1">Currently, it takes approximately 222 days to obtain a building permit in Denver as of July 2026.</cite> Another 2026 report puts major residential at <cite index="83-1">about 216 days</cite>, and another at <cite index="87-1">closer to 244 days</cite>.

What counts as "major residential": <cite index="82-1">Which are defined by the city as Home Additions, Whole Home Remodels, ADUs, and custom home builds. Smaller projects will recieve a faster plan review time.</cite>

**The honest advice that builds enormous trust:** <cite index="87-1">My best advice is to wait. Don't buy your materials until your permit is in hand. It's tempting to order those custom cabinets early, but a permit delay or change could leave you with expensive items you can't use.</cite>

**Other Denver-specific friction:**
- Zoning gates everything: <cite index="88-1">A building permit cannot issue until zoning approval confirms the proposed use and form are lawful. Projects in zone districts with design review — including the General Urban neighborhood context — require CPD Urban Design review before permit submission, adding 4–12 weeks depending on complexity.</cite>
- Revision cycles compound: <cite index="86-1">Each revision cycle can add one to three weeks to the timeline. The most common reason for plan review comments in Denver residential projects is incomplete drawings that do not show enough detail for the reviewer to verify code compliance.</cite>
- Sequencing trap: <cite index="140-1">Denver also notes that beginning May 22, 2025, certain trade permit applications for new construction and addition projects require an issued Residential Construction Permit first.</cite>
- Separate sewer use permit is required alongside zoning and building permits for ADUs. <cite index="90-1">Apply for zoning, building, sewer use and other permits</cite>
- Code edition: <cite index="136-1">Denver adopted new building and fire codes on June 13, 2025, incorporating the 2024 International Codes (including 2024 IRC), while using the 2021 IECC for energy with limited amendments. Always confirm what edition your permit will be reviewed under at the time you submit.</cite>
- Construction waste: <cite index="85-1">The city approved updates to the Universal Recycling and Composting Ordinance (known on the ballot as "Waste No More") originally passed in 2022. The ordinance covers multi-family residential buildings, food businesses, events, and non-residential construction/demolition.</cite> <cite index="85-1">The enforcement date is September 1, 2026.</cite> ⚠ Confirm exact applicability to single-family remodel demolition before publishing — the scope language is specific.
- Denver publishes actual numbers: <cite index="85-1">Denver's website now publishes average review times for building and zoning permits via a dashboard.</cite> **Link to it and cite it live.** That's the credibility move.

**Landmark Preservation (Denver's historic districts):**
<cite index="46-1">Denver's local designation program requires a design review process for exterior changes that require a permit (this includes building permit, curb cut permit, demolition permit, revocable permit, zoning construction permit and request for zoning lot amendment)</cite>. <cite index="54-1">No permit for exterior alteration, addition, new construction, or demolition of a locally designated property can be issued without a COA</cite>.

What's in and what's out — this is the useful bit for a kitchen/bath company:
- Requires review: <cite index="46-1">roof replacement or modification, window or door replacement, additions, retaining walls, installing solar panels, or new siding</cite>
- Does not: <cite index="46-1">painting your house the color of your choice, installing storm windows or security doors, interior remolding, plantings</cite>
- Scope nuance for remodelers: <cite index="54-1">Denver kitchen and bathroom remodel contractors face restrictions primarily on exterior penetrations (venting, egress windows) rather than interior scope.</cite> **That's a great, specific line** — the bath remodel is fine until you need a new exhaust vent through a historic façade or an egress window in a landmarked basement.
- Demolition reality: <cite index="46-1">Contributing (or historic) structures may only be demolished if economic hardship is established at a public hearing of the Landmark Commission. This is quite rare.</cite>

**Denver ADUs — allowed by right, with a trap:**
<cite index="96-1">As of a citywide zoning update adopted in December 2024, ADUs are allowed by right in every residential zoning district in Denver.</cite> <cite index="96-1">Denver's current ADU rules allow attached or detached units up to 1,000 square feet, with detached ADUs capped at 24 feet in height. The city does not require additional off-street parking for an ADU, and there is no owner occupancy requirement</cite>. Plus: <cite index="98-1">Detached ADUs must be within the rear 35% of the lot with a minimum 5-foot rear setback.</cite>

⚠ **Source conflict to resolve before publishing.** One 2026 source states <cite index="91-1">Denver requires owner occupancy. Either the primary home or the ADU must be the owner's principal residence.</cite> This appears to be outdated — HB24-1152 and Denver's Dec 2024 amendment both point the other way. Confirm directly with Denver CPD before you publish anything about owner-occupancy.

**The ADU hidden problem worth a whole page:** <cite index="92-1">The permitting process for ADUs on properties within a site development plan (SDP) or Planning Building Group (Former Chapter 59's version of an SDP) requires extra layers of review and a full SDP amendment because of how these properties were originally approved and developed.</cite> Almost no homeowner knows whether their lot is inside an old SDP or a Chapter 59 PBG. Summit checking that *before* design is a concrete, demonstrable service.

Reality check on cost, useful for qualifying leads: <cite index="91-1">Construction costs typically run $236,000 to $310,000 depending on size and finishes.</cite>

## 2.2 Jefferson County (unincorporated) — the layer that catches everyone

**⭐ First, the thing to lead with everywhere: the address problem.**

An enormous share of addresses that read "Morrison, CO," "Golden, CO," or "Evergreen, CO" are **not inside those municipalities**. They're unincorporated Jefferson County, which means a completely different building department, a different hazard overlay, different licensing, and — as of July 2026 — a wildfire code that the cities don't have.

Jefferson County itself directs people to check: <cite index="128-1">If you aren't sure whether your project is located within Golden city limits, visit the Jefferson County Address Wizard, enter the address, select the address you are researching, and scroll to the bottom of the page for "Municipal Status."</cite> Confirmed on the foothills side: <cite index="67-1">The town of Golden has its own municipal boundary; many surrounding foothills areas, including large parts of Morrison, are unincorporated Jefferson County and likely to fall in the overlay. Evergreen is split between Jefferson County and Clear Creek County, so an Evergreen address may fall under either county's requirements.</cite>

**Content angle — make this an interactive or at minimum a prominent callout on the Morrison and Golden pages:** "Your Mail Says Morrison. Your Building Department Might Not." This single piece of clarity will outrank a lot of thin competitor content, and it's exactly the kind of thing that makes a homeowner call you instead of the next guy.

### ⭐ The Designated Dipping Bedrock Area (DDBA) — your best geology story

This is a mapped, county-official hazard zone and it's *specific to the hogback corridor* Summit serves. Nobody in this market is writing about it well.

<cite index="56-1">The Designated Dipping Bedrock Area (DDBA) defines an area of Jefferson County where heaving bedrock is possible under certain geological and human-influenced conditions. The conditions warrant special consideration in all phases of development, including site exploration and evaluation, facilities, design, construction, and subsequent maintenance.</cite>

**Why it's worse than ordinary expansive soil:** <cite index="56-1">Compared to damage caused by flat-lying expansive soils and bedrock, which are found to the east over much of the Denver metropolitan area, heaving bedrock problems are more complex in nature and difficult to predict, and the resulting damage is often more localized and destructive.</cite> And the cost: <cite index="56-1">logical hazard is responsible for tens of millions of dollars in excess maintenance costs to County taxpayers and homeowners.</cite>

**Where it is — genuinely useful geography:** <cite index="56-1">The western boundary corresponds to the contact between the Graneros Shale and underlying Dakota Sandstone on the eastern dip slope of the Hogback ridge or near Golden where these units are missing due to faulting, to the mapped location of the Golden Fault. The eastern boundary corresponds roughly to the eastern extent of the bedrock which dips at greater than 30 degrees from horizontal. Bedrock layers underlying the DDBA dip to the east or northeast at 30 to 90 degrees from horizontal.</cite>

**The detail that turns this into remodel content — and it's dramatic:** USGS research found <cite index="62-1">wetting produces a distinct swelling response in the claystone strata, and it may occur repeatedly as natural precipitation or from lawn irrigation. We documented 2.5 to 7.5 cm (1 to 3 in.) of differential heaving in 24 hours triggered by sudden infiltration of water at the exposed ground surface in outcrops and at construction sites.</cite>

Read that again: **one to three inches of movement in twenty-four hours, triggered by water hitting exposed ground.** In a DDBA property, an open excavation for an addition footing, a regraded patio, or a new irrigation zone is not a cosmetic decision.

The visual everyone recognizes: <cite index="55-1">This "roller-coaster" road is the result of uneven expansion and heaving of steeply dipping bedrock layers.</cite> If Summit shoots that on a local DDBA street, that photo anchors the entire page. **This is a perfect fit for your on-site photography differentiator** — nobody else has original photos of local heave damage.

Neighborhoods locally associated with moving soils (⚠ secondary source, verify each against the official Jeffco DDBA map before naming any in copy): <cite index="59-1">Friendly Hills, Trappers Glenn, Spring Hill, Belleview Acres, Harriman Park, Lakehurst, Foothills Green, Country West, Dancing Willows, West Meadows, Ken Caryl Ranch, Powderhorn</cite>. Jeffco publishes the authoritative map; use it, cite it, link it.

**Content angles:**
- "Why We Won't Pour Your Addition Footing Without a Soils Report in [Morrison/Golden]"
- "The Sprinkler Zone That Cracked a Foundation"
- "Drainage Is Not Landscaping — It's Structural, West of Kipling"

### ⭐ The Jefferson County Wildfire Resiliency Code — brand new, July 1, 2026

This is the freshest, most actionable, most under-covered item in the entire research pass. It took effect ten weeks ago and it **directly governs remodel scope** in unincorporated Jeffco.

**The state mandate:** <cite index="63-1">Pursuant to state law, the Colorado Wildfire Resiliency Code Board adopted the 2025 Colorado Wildfire Resiliency Code (CWRC) on July 1, 2025. The CWRC includes requirements for structure hardening, defensible space, and WUI boundary mapping.</cite> <cite index="63-1">Governing bodies such as Jefferson County must adopt the CWRC or a code that meets or exceeds the minimum standards in the CWRC by April 1, 2026, and enforce the requirements by July 1, 2026.</cite>

Crucially for remodelers: <cite index="66-1">On July 1, 2025, the state adopted the CWRC, which the department of fire prevention and control describes as "a state minimum code applying to new construction and significant exterior alterations and additions within the Wildland Urban Interface." It is not a retrofit code.</cite> **"Significant exterior alterations and additions" is Summit's entire business.**

**Jeffco's version:** <cite index="63-1">On March 10, 2026, the Jefferson County Board of County Commissioners (BCC) approved the WRC, updates to Zoning Resolution Section 39 Wildland Urban Interface Overlay District, and a new Wildland Urban Interface (WUI) Overlay District map.</cite> <cite index="68-1">Jefferson County, for example, runs its own WRC with a Class 1 / Class 2 overlay map for unincorporated areas.</cite>

**What it actually requires — put this in a table:** <cite index="65-1">Structure hardening requirements include roofing classified as Class A, siding and deck framing materials that limit fire ignition, deck surfaces constructed of noncombustible materials, and performance standards for ventilation openings to prevent ember intrusion. In addition, fencing within eight feet of structures must be constructed of nonflammable materials. Select highly flammable plants are prohibited within 30 feet of structures such as junipers.</cite>

**The two process requirements that surprise people:** <cite index="64-1">someone can build a structure but must hire a certified fire specialist to verify the structure has a "defensible space" — cleared and thinned vegetation around the structure — and that roads and driveways meet access standards so emergency vehicles can reach the location. These defensible space permits expire every three years, and property owners are legally required to maintain their clearings from vegetation growth.</cite>

A third-party certified inspection in the permit path, plus a *recurring* obligation. Jeffco even publishes the list: <cite index="63-1">Certified Wildfire Mitigation Specialists for Defensible Space Inspections (PDF)</cite> and a <cite index="63-1">Defensible Space Certification for Siding Permit (PDF)</cite> — note that last one: **a siding permit triggers a defensible space certification.** That is a remodel job.

**What it does NOT cover — equally important, and a great disambiguation piece:** <cite index="67-1">The WRC only applies to unincorporated Jefferson County, not to homes inside city limits of Golden, Lakewood, Arvada, Wheat Ridge, or other incorporated municipalities</cite>. And: <cite index="69-1">The code is not retroactive.</cite> <cite index="69-1">Most existing homeowners won't be required to immediately make changes to their property simply because the code is now in effect.</cite>

**The risk framing:** <cite index="65-1">Jeffco ranks second out of Colorado's 64 counties when it comes to wildfire risk. Our wildfire risk is 98% above average for counties across the country.</cite> And the angle homeowners care about most: <cite index="65-1">These efforts can also positively impact skyrocketing homeowner insurance rates driven by both hail and wildfire, especially in our mountain communities.</cite> **Insurance is the emotional hook here, not code compliance.** Lead with "this might help you keep your policy," not "the county says you must."

## 3.2 Arvada

**⭐ The framing that should anchor this page: Arvada is three cities.**

A competitor already articulated this well, which tells you the angle works — and tells you Summit needs to go deeper: <cite index="79-1">Arvada is three cities in one, and which one your house belongs to decides the project. Then there's west Arvada Candelas, Leyden Rock, and the newer development toward the foothills where homes are recent, systems are modern, and remodeling is about layout and finish rather than repair. **⭐ The historic district hurdle — three districts, not one.**

The three historic districts were NRHP-listed in 1998 and 1999</cite>. The third is Reno Park.

**The requirement, stated hard by the city:** <cite index="74-1">Any modification, addition, restoration, or redevelopment in the historic district or zoned Olde Town (OT) must first obtain a Certificate of Compliance with the Olde Town Design Guidelines prior to construction.</cite>

**What the application actually takes** — this is the operational detail that proves Summit has done it: <cite index="77-1">The application consists of 4 elements: the application and fee, a written narrative, graphic illustrations, and a compliance analysis. And the narrative requirements: <cite index="77-1">If any material will be removed, note the material, color and location. ... Explain how the change will vary the appearance of the existing structure. If there will be no appearance change it should be noted in the narrative. ... Explain how the proposal is in compliance with the design guidelines.</cite>

That's a written compliance analysis, not a form. A homeowner cannot produce that. **That is the sales argument.**

**Geography of the districts (put a map on the page):**

## 3.3 Wheat Ridge

**⭐ This is the strongest "era of homes" page in the set, because the numbers are extraordinary.**

<cite index="13-1">Wheat Ridge has an older housing stock than many other Denver-area suburbs. According to the city, nearly 80% of single-family homes were built between 1940 and 1979, and only 12% were built in 1980 or later as of 2016.</cite> A corroborating breakdown: <cite index="16-1">More than seventy percent of Wheat Ridge's housing was built between 1940 and 1979, split roughly thirty-eight percent in the 1940s and 1950s and thirty-three percent in the 1960s and 1970s. Postwar brick ranches are the defining product here.</cite>

**80% of the housing stock is 45+ years old.** That's the whole page in one statistic, and Summit should own it.

**⭐ The lot pattern is a genuinely distinctive local story.** <cite index="16-1">The area's history as a market-garden and carnation-greenhouse district left an unusual pattern: large lots with older, smaller houses on them. That combination drives both the local scrape-and-replace market and the accessory dwelling unit market, and it means lot value can be a bigger factor here than house condition.</cite>

This is a fantastic, specific piece of local history that leads directly to a business conversation: in Wheat Ridge more than anywhere else in the six markets, the question is "remodel, add on, ADU, or scrape?" — and the answer depends on a lot pattern created by 1940s carnation greenhouses. **Write that piece.** Nobody has.

**Mid-century specifics — Paramount Heights as a case study.** <cite index="15-1">Paramount Heights is a mid-century modern neighborhood in Wheat Ridge, Colorado, built mostly between 1954 and the early 1970s on the rise above Crown Hill Lake. It is roughly [X] homes, mostly ranches, on lots that run close to a third of an acre</cite> with <cite index="15-1">flat roofs and the long low rooflines</cite> and some <cite index="15-1">International Style homes</cite>. The renovation cautions listed in §1.7 above are all drawn from this neighborhood and apply across Wheat Ridge's postwar stock.

Also worth noting for the east side: <cite index="10-1">Homes in East Wheat Ridge range from older brick bungalows, built in the first half of the 20th century, to sizeable contemporary builds that have been added over the past several years.</cite> And the lot quirk again, from a local agent: <cite index="10-1">"There can be some funky lot sizes here"</cite>.

**⭐ The water-fee trap — Wheat Ridge has multiple water providers and double fees.**

<cite index="124-1">Wheat Ridge has multiple water providers: Consolidated Mutual Water and Wheat Ridge Water.</cite> Coverage split: <cite index="117-1">The Wheat Ridge Water District serves areas east of Independence Court, as well as Mountain View, Lakeside, and neighborhoods south of I‑70 between Carr and Garrison. And here's the fee stack that blindsides people — straight from the district's own January 2026 rate resolution: <cite index="125-1">These fees are in addition to Denver Water System Development Charges which also apply to the area of the Wheat Ridge Water District.</cite>

**Two separate organizations, two separate charges, neither of them the City of Wheat Ridge.** If an addition or ADU requires a larger tap or meter, that's district tap charges *plus* Denver Water SDCs. Aging infrastructure context: the Wheat Ridge Water District is <cite index="117-1">replac[ing] aging cast-iron water mains with modern ductile-iron and PVC lines — an effort expected</cite> to cover about 20 miles of pipe. Useful for framing why a service line replacement might be smart to do during a remodel.

**City planning context:** <cite index="12-1">The City's 2005 Neighborhood Revitalization Strategy called for an increase in housing options to attract a wider range of household types. ## 3.4 Lakewood

**⭐ This page has the single most newsworthy, most current content opportunity in the entire set. Five months old.**

### The April 2026 zoning repeal

<cite index="29-1">Lakewood voters decisively repealed the city's new zoning laws on Tuesday night, striking down a change that would have allowed more duplexes, triplexes and other multi-unit housing across the city's suburban neighborhoods. As of 10 p.m., the repeal effort was crushing the pro-density campaign with about 65% of the vote.</cite>

**The timeline, which matters a lot:** <cite index="36-1">Proposed changes to the city's zoning code hit councilmembers' desks in February 2025. After months of heated discussions, the council approved them in October. The zoning changes went into effect in January but were paused after the petition to put them on the ballot for voters to decide was deemed sufficient.</cite> <cite index="32-1">About 16,700 residents voted to rebuff the proposed changes, instead sticking with the zoning enacted around 2014.</cite>

**So: Lakewood is operating on a ~2014 zoning code.** Four ordinances (2025-27 through 2025-30) were repealed, covering <cite index="27-1">residential building and site design standards; institutional, mixed use, commercial, and light industrial building and site design standards; parking and loading standards; sign standards; wireless services and communications; historic preservation; nonconformities; sustainable development; and definitions and interpretations</cite> plus <cite index="28-1">the establishment of zone districts</cite> and <cite index="28-1">repealing the existing zoning map, replacing it with the new zoning map, and rezoning properties thereby</cite>.

**What Lakewood homeowners lost that they may not realize:** the repealed code <cite index="40-1">align[ed] with state law (HB 24-1152) by eliminating some barriers to feasibility like excessive parking requirements, while going further—allowing larger ADUs, up to 1,400sf</cite>. It also <cite index="36-1">aimed to increase housing options by removing the term "single-family zoning" from city documents. In its place, the city adopted the term "residential dwellings" — an umbrella category encompassing single-family homes, duplexes, triplexes and townhomes.</cite> And it would have <cite index="40-1">permitted</cite> small neighborhood retail up to 750 sf in residential neighborhoods.

**⭐ The live uncertainty — this is the "hidden problem" for anyone planning a Lakewood ADU right now.**

<cite index="35-1">Councilmember Roger Low said that by going back to the old zoning rules, Lakewood will fall out of compliance with HB24-1304.</cite> <cite index="26-1">The new zoning policies had been on pause and now are set to be discarded.</cite> Council's position: <cite index="35-1">"As a council, we will have to go back to the drawing board to figure out our next steps."</cite>

But the picture is genuinely contested, and Summit should present it that way. The mayor has said the old code already complied: <cite index="41-1">"It's important to understand that the city's current zone districts for neighborhoods with single family homes already allow accessory dwelling units, duplexes and more, and several neighborhoods already have them," Mayor Wendi Strom said.</cite>

**The honest content play:** "Building an ADU in Lakewood After the April 2026 Repeal: What We Know, What's Unsettled, and How We'd Approach Your Project." Lay out the facts neutrally, note that the code is in flux, and commit to verifying current rules for the specific address before design. That's a page that will get bookmarked, linked, and cited — and it positions Summit as the steady hand while the city figures itself out.

⚠ **Time-sensitive.** Council has been "back to the drawing board" since April. Check for new ordinances before publishing and set a calendar reminder to re-verify quarterly. Date-stamp the page prominently.

⚠ **Stay scrupulously neutral.** This was a 65/35 local fight with real anger on both sides, and Summit's customers voted both ways. Report the rules. Do not editorialize about density.

### The prior chapter (useful background, shows depth)

<cite index="22-1">Lakewood voters passed a controversial rule that limits new residential construction to 1 percent of the city's existing housing stock each year</cite> in July 2019. Then <cite index="17-1">A new state law forbids cities like Lakewood from enforcing what it calls "anti-growth" policies.</cite> <cite index="17-1">The council voted 8-3 to put an expiration date on the city's growth law, setting it to disappear in two years.</cite>

### ⭐ Lakewood's water situation is genuinely unique — 20 districts

This is a remarkable and under-reported fact, and it has direct remodel consequences.

<cite index="117-1">Lakewood gets most of its water from Denver Water and therefore follows those drought restrictions. That supply is then distributed through 20 independent special districts across the city, with Consolidated Mutual Water and the Green Mountain Water and Sanitation District serving the largest number of customers.</cite> <cite index="117-1">The city itself does not manage the water infrastructure — those responsibilities remain with the independent districts.</cite>

Why: <cite index="117-1">the structure dates back to before Lakewood incorporated in 1969, when roughly 90,000 residents relied on self‑formed special districts for water service. When the city was established, it did not take over those systems. Lakewood has assumed control of only one water and sewer district, which serves about 7,000 people.</cite>

Named districts to reference: <cite index="119-1">Consolidated Mutual Water Company; Daniels Sanitation District; East Jefferson County Sanitation District; East Lakewood Sanitation District</cite>, plus <cite index="122-1">Bancroft-Clover Water and Sanitation District... a special district providing water and wastewater service to over 66,000 people in portions of southeastern Lakewood and unincorporated Jefferson county for nearly 70 years</cite> and <cite index="121-1">Northwest Lakewood Sanitation District... organized in 1954</cite>.

**And the fee-splitting detail that matters:** <cite index="122-1">Bancroft Clover does not quote or collect water tap fees on behalf of Denver Water. You may contact Denver Water at 303-628-6100</cite>. Again — two separate bills, and the homeowner has to chase both.

**Content angle:** "Who Actually Supplies Your Water in Lakewood? (There Are 20 Possible Answers, and It Changes Your Remodel Budget.)" Make it an address-lookup guide pointing to jMap. This is pure, practical, unsexy value — which is exactly what earns authority.

**Housing era.** Postwar ranches and split-levels, same vintage as Arvada's middle: <cite index="79-1">the post-war boom: ranches and split-levels built through the 1950s, 60s, and 70s</cite>. West Lakewood approaches the hogback — check DDBA.

## 3.5 Morrison

**⭐ Lead with the jurisdiction question. It's the most useful thing you can tell a Morrison homeowner.**

The Town of Morrison is very small, and most "Morrison" addresses in the foothills are unincorporated Jefferson County: <cite index="67-1">many surrounding foothills areas, including large parts of Morrison, are unincorporated Jefferson County and likely to fall in the overlay</cite>.

**Practical consequence:** if the address is unincorporated, the governing rules are the Jeffco set — DDBA, the Wildfire Resiliency Code (effective July 1, 2026), special-district water/sewer, and likely OWTS/well. If it's inside town limits, a completely different and much smaller building department applies. **Build an address-check callout at the top of this page.** ⚠ Verify the specific unincorporated areas before naming neighborhoods; use the Jeffco Address Wizard as the authority and link it.

### If it IS the Town of Morrison

**A brand-new land use code.** <cite index="100-1">The new regulations split the town into different zoning districts and define allowed uses in those areas. It also includes development standards and outlines development review procedures. Among the highlights: The new regulations establish a specific zoning district for Morrison's historic core, drop four feet from the maximum allowed building height there and require developers in that area to get more creative in their building design.</cite> Also: <cite index="100-1">It also changes the previous agricultural zoning to rural residential, eliminating restrictions on private property within those areas.</cite>

**A four-foot height reduction in the historic core** is a very concrete constraint — it can be the difference between a pop-top being feasible or not.

And more is coming: <cite index="100-1">The newly adopted revisions are not the last word on Morrison's land use... there will be a second phase of changes. Those revisions will look more closely at subdivision standards, stormwater and drainage, and building design in residential districts.</cite> ⚠ **Check whether Phase 2 has been adopted.** If residential building design standards are now in place, that's a significant update and a timely piece.

**The small-town operational reality** — a detail that signals real familiarity: <cite index="106-1">PLEASE NOTE: Inspection requests must be submitted by 4:00 PM on the business day prior to the requested date.</cite> No same-day inspections. That shapes a schedule.

The town is old: <cite index="101-1">The Town of Morrison was platted in 1874</cite>, and the <cite index="99-1">Morrison Historic District</cite> is a defined and actively protected asset — <cite index="99-1">Policy HD 3: Raise public awareness about the importance and value of the Morrison Historic District.</cite>

### ⭐ The Bear Creek floodplain — the hardest technical constraint in the six markets

Morrison sits <cite index="99-1">at the bottom of winding Bear Creek Canyon</cite>, and the town is actively working to <cite index="99-1">improve a working relationship with the Mile High Flood District</cite> and to <cite index="99-1">Continue efforts to relocate the town maintenance equipment storage building and maintenance shops out of the Bear Creek floodplain.</cite> When the town can't get its own building out of the floodplain, that tells a homeowner something.

**The rule that governs a Morrison addition** — from Colorado Water Conservation Board floodplain rules: <cite index="103-1">New and Substantially Changed residential structures, and Additions to existing residential structures shall be constructed with the lowest floor, including basements, placed with a minimum of one foot of freeboard above the Base Flood Elevation.</cite>

**Read the words "and Additions to existing residential structures."** In a mapped floodplain, an addition's lowest floor — *including basement* — has to sit a foot above Base Flood Elevation. That can make a walkout basement addition impossible, force a raised floor that doesn't match the existing house, or kill a project outright at the feasibility stage. **This is exactly the kind of thing a homeowner finds out after paying for design, and exactly what Summit can catch on day one.**

Related trap: <cite index="103-1">No Community shall issue a permit for the construction of a new structure on a property removed from the floodplain by the issuance of a FEMA Letter of Map Revision Based on Fill (LOMR-F)</cite> below the required elevation — so "we got it removed from the floodplain" isn't the end of the story.

Also relevant: Morrison's own plan notes <cite index="99-1">Identify and educate residents on effective measures to mitigate the risk of wildland fires</cite> and <cite index="99-1">Identify funding resources to bury utility infrastructure underground in high fire danger</cite> areas.

**Page spine:** are you in the Town or the County? → (County) WRC + DDBA + special districts + likely septic/well → (Town) new land use code, historic core height limit, Bear Creek floodplain freeboard → foothills access/logistics.

⚠ **Gaps to fill for this page:** OWTS/septic rules via Jeffco Public Health; well permitting via Colorado Division of Water Resources; driveway grade and emergency access standards; design snow and wind loads at elevation; rockfall and steep-slope hazard overlays. Morrison is the page where these matter most and I didn't reach them.

## 3.6 Golden

**⭐ The historic preservation process here is stricter and more formal than anywhere else in the six markets — and the 50-year rule is the hook.**

**The line that should be on the page verbatim-in-spirit:** <cite index="45-1">If the building is over 50 years old it might be designated or part of a district.</cite> That reframes it — a homeowner thinks "historic" means Victorian. In Golden it can mean a 1970s house.

**The requirement:** <cite index="45-1">Most exterior alterations to a building within the established historic districts or a locally designated site must have a Certificate of Appropriateness (COA) review by the Historic Preservation Board.</cite> <cite index="45-1">Renovating a building within one of Golden's historic districts comes with opportunities and constraints.</cite>

**The three districts, by name:** <cite index="48-1">The City has three locally designated historic districts, the 8th and 9th Street District, the 12th Street District, and the East Street District and many individually designated structures.</cite>

**Program depth and legal citation** (this is the kind of specificity that reads as authority): <cite index="47-1">Golden's preservation program began in 1983 with the designation of the 12th Street Historic District as a National Register Historic District. Golden is a Certified Local Government with the State of Colorado and the United States Department of the Interior. The current preservation program was last updated significantly in 2008 with guidelines for certificates of appropriateness reviews and procedures located in Chapter 18.58 of the Golden Municipal Code.</cite>

**⭐ The process detail that proves Summit has actually been through it.** This is a real 2025 agenda item, and it's gold for content:

<cite index="48-1">The Historic Preservation Board of the City of Golden will hold a Public Hearing at a meeting on Wednesday, February 12, 2025 at 6:30 P.M. at Golden City Hall, 911 10th Street, Golden, Colorado to review a certificate of appropriateness for a rear addition to 1815 East Street in the East Street Historic District. The next step is to apply for a Form Zone Site Plan to review zoning.</cite>

Three things a homeowner learns from that sentence: **a rear addition** — not a façade change — **goes to a public evening hearing**; there's a **public comment window** where neighbors can weigh in (<cite index="48-1">All interested persons are invited to comment by written communication to the Planning Department</cite>); and **the COA isn't the end** — after it, you still apply for a Form Zone Site Plan for zoning review. Two sequential approvals before a building permit.

**Content angle:** "A Rear Addition in Golden Goes to a Public Hearing. Here's How We Prepare for It." That's a page that converts, because it turns an intimidating unknown into a managed process.

Also mention: <cite index="45-1">Federal and state tax laws provide tax incentives for historic preservation projects that follow the Secretary of the Interior's Standards for Rehabilitation. Preservation tax credit applications are handled through History Colorado.</cite> A builder who can steer a client toward a state historic tax credit is providing real financial value. ⚠ Verify current Colorado credit terms and residential eligibility with History Colorado before making any claims.

**Golden's contractor licensing is unusual — worth a short factual note.** <cite index="128-1">The City of Golden requires that ALL licensed contractors be identified on building permits before the permit can be issued or inspections occur.</cite> But: <cite index="128-1">While the City of Golden requires state license information for plumbers and electricians who work on projects in our city, we DO NOT administer or require testing for general trade licenses.</cite> And note the state's spot checks: <cite index="128-1">The State of Colorado mandates that inspectors may randomly check for state electrical and plumbing license compliance at commercial and multi-family projects.</cite>

**Geology — Golden is where the DDBA gets complicated.** The DDBA's western boundary is defined partly by the Golden Fault: <cite index="56-1">near Golden where these units are missing due to faulting, to the mapped location of the Golden Fault</cite>. Golden sits at the intersection of the hogback, the fault, and the Clear Creek corridor. Every one of those is a foundation-design input.

**Growth policy note:** Golden was one of the cities with a growth cap repealed by state law, and <cite index="19-1">Golden, another city with a cap, punted on Tuesday when its City Council held a closed-door meeting with their attorney and then didn't take any action.</cite> ⚠ Check current status before publishing — this was 2023.

**Page spine:** are you inside city limits? → is your home 50+ years old / in a district? → the COA public hearing process → the Form Zone Site Plan second step → historic tax credits → DDBA and Clear Creek → era findings.
